How I turn ambiguity into design decisions

Most projects don’t start with clarity. They start with scattered ideas, incomplete requirements, conflicting opinions and a lot of unanswered questions.

Over time I’ve built a process for moving from that uncertainty to a confident decision, and AI has become part of it. Not to make the decisions for me, but to help me think faster, explore more directions and get to a shippable answer sooner. Here’s how it shows up.

Making sense of the chaos

Every project starts with the same goal: getting to clarity before I open Figma.

  • Claude
  • ChatGPT
  • Stakeholder research

I use Claude and ChatGPT to break down PRDs, meeting notes and stakeholder feedback, surface the gaps and find the questions nobody has answered yet. That synthesis goes back into research and stakeholder workshops, rather than skipping straight to screens.

Exploring before committing

  • Figma Make

Once the problem is clear, Figma Make turns ideas into wireframes and flows in minutes instead of hours.

The biggest advantage isn’t speed, it’s flexibility. When a new direction costs ten minutes instead of an afternoon, I explore more of them before committing to one. That’s how tasteX went from concept to a deployed UAT build in under 5 months.

Refining the decision, not just the screen

  • Claude
  • Figma AI

This is where most of the real thinking happens.

I use Claude as a design-thinking partner: pressure-testing assumptions, checking flows against established heuristics and arguing the other side of a decision before I commit. Figma AI’s auto-layout and variant generation handle the repetitive structural work, so I can stay on the product decision underneath it.

Shipping faster, without skipping steps

  • Codex
  • Claude Code
  • GitHub Copilot

When I’m also driving implementation, Codex and GitHub Copilot speed up code generation and Claude Code handles deployment and CI/CD. I direct an AI-orchestrated pipeline rather than working through it screen by screen.

It’s the difference between a three-portal product taking a traditional year to build and one that reached UAT in under five months.

What AI has actually changed

The biggest change isn’t that I design faster. It’s that I get stuck less.

I spend less time stalled at the start of a problem and more time refining the parts that matter, and I can explore more directions before I have to commit to one.

The tools will keep changing. The advantage is staying curious enough to keep changing with them.
